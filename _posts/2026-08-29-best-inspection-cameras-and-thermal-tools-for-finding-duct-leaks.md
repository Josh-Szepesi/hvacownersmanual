Your HVAC system might be running fine on paper, but leaking ducts can silently drain 20 to 30 percent of your conditioned air into attics, crawlspaces, and wall cavities before it ever reaches a living space. The [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/duct-sealing) lists duct leakage among the most significant and most underaddressed sources of energy waste in American homes. You feel the results every month on your utility bill and every summer afternoon when one room never reaches the thermostat setpoint despite the system running nonstop.

The frustrating part is that most meaningful duct leaks are invisible. A supply boot separated from the ceiling drywall by a quarter inch, a flex duct fitting where the inner sleeve has pulled back from the collar, or a mastic-sealed seam that cracked during construction - none of these show up in a casual visual walkthrough. You need diagnostic tools that see what your eyes cannot.

Two categories of tools give homeowners real diagnostic capability without contractor-level investment: thermal imaging cameras, which detect surface temperature anomalies caused by air leakage, and inspection cameras (borescopes), which let you see inside duct interiors to confirm damage directly. Neither replaces a professional blower door and duct blaster test, but together they produce a concrete diagnostic picture before you spend money on repairs or professional services. The right tools make this process accessible to any homeowner willing to spend an afternoon on it.

## Why Duct Leaks Are Harder to Find Than You Think

Duct systems sprawl across attics, crawlspaces, basements, and interior wall chases. Most of that network is physically inaccessible without cutting into ceilings or floors. Even the sections you can reach often hide their leaks at seams, boot connections, and branch takeoffs that look intact from the outside.

Standard visual inspection catches obvious failures: a completely disconnected flex duct section, a register sitting unconnected on the floor, or a boot so separated from the ceiling that daylight shows around the edges. Those failures are real, but they represent a small fraction of total duct leakage. The bigger problem is incremental leakage at joints that appear sealed. Original mastic application dries and cracks over years of thermal cycling. Cloth-backed duct tape loses adhesion within a few seasons. Flex duct connections can slip at the collar from years of vibration and pressure cycling.

[ENERGY STAR's guidance on duct sealing](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/seal_and_insulate/ducts) identifies connections at plenums, boots, and branch fittings as the highest-probability leak locations in a residential duct system. That finding is useful because it tells you exactly where to focus your diagnostic effort. A thermal camera aimed at ceiling boot connections during a heating or cooling cycle will reveal anomalies that are completely invisible to the naked eye.

The other challenge with duct leakage is its compounding impact. A duct system with several small leaks in unconditioned space loses efficiency in a way that's non-linear, because each leak changes the pressure balance of the entire system. Return-side leaks pull unconditioned attic air or crawlspace air into the return stream, degrading air quality and raising humidity. Supply-side leaks dump conditioned air into unconditioned cavities, directly wasting the energy your equipment consumed to produce it. Both scenarios force longer run times and higher equipment wear. Identifying and sealing even the most significant leaks produces measurable improvements in both comfort and energy bills.

## Thermal Imaging Cameras for Duct Leak Detection

Thermal cameras detect infrared radiation emitted by surfaces. They do not see through walls, but they reveal surface temperature differences caused by what is happening behind or inside those surfaces. A supply duct running through an unconditioned attic and leaking at a fitting creates a temperature anomaly at the ceiling below. A boot connection that has separated at the drywall shows a distinct cold ring (during cooling season) or warm ring (during heating season) around the register opening.

Two specifications matter most when choosing a thermal camera for duct inspection work.

**Native IR resolution** determines how much spatial detail the thermal image contains. Consumer phone-attached cameras typically use 160x120 native sensors. The apparent resolution number in marketing copy is often higher because of interpolation - what matters is the native sensor. The [Flir One Pro - Thermal Imaging Camera for iOS Smartphones](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DPXWFD12?tag=hvacowners-20) uses a 160x120 native IR sensor with 480x360 Super Resolution output. It connects via USB-C and is compatible with iPhone 15 and newer devices.

**Thermal sensitivity (NETD)** is the minimum temperature difference the camera can reliably detect. Lower values mean the camera catches more subtle anomalies. A camera with 150 mK NETD will show temperature differences that a 300 mK unit misses entirely. For duct inspection, this matters when boot connection leaks are subtle or when you're scanning during milder weather with a smaller temperature differential.

## Inspection Cameras: Getting Inside Your Ductwork

A thermal camera locates the problem from the outside. A borescope inspects from the inside. For duct work specifically, interior inspection answers the "why" and "how bad" questions that thermal imaging raises but cannot fully answer on its own.

The [Klein Tools ET21 WiFi Borescope](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GKBXR1ZR?tag=hvacowners-20) connects wirelessly to iOS or Android devices, which removes the phone-compatibility constraint that limits some competing tools. The IP67 waterproof rating matters for crawlspace inspections where condensation and groundwater are routine. The adjustable LED lighting around the camera lens is a genuinely useful feature - duct interiors are dark, and fixed lighting creates glare off metallic surfaces that washes out the detail you're looking for. Adjustable lighting lets you dial in a better exposure to see lining tears, joint separations, and collapsed sections.

Borescopes for duct inspection work best when you have a clear point of entry. Supply registers are the primary access point, and a 6-inch supply register gives you enough clearance to pass the flexible camera head 4 to 8 feet into a duct run and navigate one or two gentle bends. You will not reach every section of your system from a single register, but you will get visual confirmation of the highest-risk areas: the flex duct connection at its collar, the condition of older metal ductwork near the main trunk, and the state of duct lining in areas your thermal scan flagged.

What to look for on the borescope feed: visible gaps at seam connections, the inner sleeve of flex duct pulling away from the collar fitting (it looks like a gathered fabric sleeve moving back from a metal ring), collapsed or kinked duct sections, and any visible holes or tears in the inner liner. These visual findings, cross-referenced with the thermal anomalies your camera identified, give you a confident diagnosis before you commit to repairs or call a contractor.

## How to Find Duct Leaks Step by Step

A systematic approach produces reliable results. Rushing the scan or skipping setup conditions leads to false negatives and wasted repair effort.

1. **Run your HVAC system for 15 to 20 minutes before scanning.** You need a meaningful temperature differential between conditioned air inside the ducts and the ambient temperature in unconditioned spaces. In summer, run the AC; in winter, run the furnace. A differential of 15 degrees or more between supply air temperature and ambient gives thermal cameras a clear enough signal to detect leakage anomalies reliably.

2. **Start at the air handler and plenum connections.** The plenum - the distribution box that feeds conditioned air from the air handler into duct branches - is the highest-pressure point in the system and typically carries the most significant leaks. Scan the plenum body, all flex duct collar connections at the trunk, and the return air box before moving to individual branch runs.

3. **Work systematically along branch runs toward registers.** Move your thermal camera slowly along the ceiling or wall surface above each duct run. Hold the camera in each position for two to three seconds before advancing. Anomalies appear as distinct color shifts in the thermal palette - a cool blue spot on a warm ceiling during cooling season, or a warm patch on a cooler surface during heating season.

4. **Mark anomalies before entering tight spaces.** When your thermal camera identifies a temperature anomaly, mark that ceiling or wall location with painter's tape immediately. Photograph the thermal image showing the anomaly against the physical location. Attic orientation is disorienting once you are inside the space, and you will lose track of which ceiling point corresponds to which duct section without physical markers.

5. **Confirm findings with the borescope at the nearest accessible register.** Insert the Klein ET21 at the register closest to a flagged anomaly. Advance the camera slowly and look for visual indicators of leakage: separated connections, torn inner lining, pulled-back flex duct inner sleeves, or collapsed sections.

6. **Verify your CO detector is functioning before inspecting near a gas furnace.** This step is non-negotiable. If you are scanning ductwork near a gas-fired furnace, your CO detector must be working and located within range. If the alarm triggers during your inspection, exit the house and call 911. Do not attempt to locate a heat exchanger crack yourself - that requires combustion analysis equipment and a licensed technician.

7. **Prioritize repairs by location and leakage severity.** Leaks in ducts running through unconditioned attics cause the most energy loss per unit area. Plenum leaks affect overall system pressure and cascade through every branch. Use your thermal survey images to sequence repairs from most impactful to least. ## Common Mistakes When Searching for Duct Leaks

### Scanning Without Adequate Temperature Differential

The most common reason a thermal scan produces inconclusive results is insufficient temperature differential. If supply air is only 5 degrees cooler than ambient room temperature, a small boot connection leak will not generate a readable surface anomaly on the ceiling. Run the system for at least 15 minutes before scanning, and choose a day when outdoor temperatures create a natural differential. Hot summer days and cold winter days are ideal conditions for this work. Mild spring or fall weather, when the system runs briefly or not at all, is the worst possible time to scan. If you cannot achieve adequate differential because of weather or schedule, reschedule rather than interpret inconclusive images.

### Focusing on Duct Runs Instead of Joints

Duct runs - the long straight sections between connection points - rarely develop leaks in the middle. Leakage concentrates at transitions: boot-to-drywall interfaces, flex duct collar connections, branch takeoffs from trunk lines, and the plenum box. Homeowners who scan methodically along the length of duct runs but skim over the joints routinely conclude their system is fine when the actual problem is at every connection they bypassed. Flip your time allocation: spend roughly 70 percent of your scanning time on joints and connection points, and 30 percent on straight runs.

### Misreading Thermal Anomalies as Definitive Duct Leaks

A thermal anomaly at the ceiling surface can mean several things: a duct connection leak, an insulation void where attic insulation is missing or thin, a cold water supply pipe running close to the surface, or a structural thermal bridge at a framing member. An anomaly that tracks linearly along a known duct path is almost certainly duct-related. An anomaly in an area with no ductwork above it should prompt an insulation check before you cut into anything. The borescope is your confirmation tool. If you cannot visually confirm a leak at the location your thermal camera flagged, explore other explanations before you commit to repairs.

### Skipping Documentation Before Starting Repairs

Many homeowners scan, identify a problem, and immediately start applying mastic without any record of what they found. After repairs, the thermal anomaly disappears - which is the goal - but so does every record of where the leak was and how significant it appeared. Photograph every anomaly before you repair it. A simple hand-drawn sketch of your duct system with numbered repair locations, cross-referenced with your thermal photos, creates a useful before-and-after record. That documentation is valuable if you later have an energy audit, sell the home, or need to compare utility costs across seasons to confirm your repairs produced the expected improvement.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I find duct leaks with just a thermal camera, without a borescope?

Yes, and for most homeowners a thermal camera alone is the right starting point. Thermal imaging identifies where leaks are likely occurring based on surface temperature anomalies, and that information is often sufficient to direct mastic and foil tape repairs to the highest-probability locations. The borescope adds interior visual confirmation, which matters most when you are dealing with flex duct in areas you cannot physically access, or when you want to distinguish between a duct leak and another cause of a thermal anomaly such as an insulation void. If budget is a constraint, start with the thermal camera. Add the borescope only if your initial thermal results are ambiguous or if you have reason to suspect significant internal duct damage such as collapsed flex lining.

### What temperature differential do I need for thermal imaging to work on ducts?

A minimum of 10 to 15 degrees Fahrenheit between duct supply air temperature and ambient temperature in the space you are scanning is the generally accepted threshold for readable thermal anomalies in building diagnostics. The greater the differential, the more clearly leaks register on the camera. Scanning on a hot summer day with the AC running, or a cold winter day with the furnace on, produces far more readable results than scanning during mild weather when the system runs briefly and supply air temperatures hover close to ambient. Manufacturer guidance for thermal cameras used in building inspection work typically cites 15 to 20 degrees as the recommended minimum for reliable diagnostic results. If weather conditions do not cooperate, reschedule rather than attempt to interpret marginal images.

### Do I need a professional blower door test even if I have done my own thermal scan?

A thermal scan tells you where leaks are likely located. A professional blower door and duct blaster test tells you how much total leakage your system has, expressed as a measurable airflow percentage. These are complementary diagnostics, not substitutes. For most homeowners whose goal is to identify and repair the most significant leaks to improve comfort and reduce energy costs, a thorough thermal and borescope survey followed by systematic mastic repairs produces most of the benefit without the professional test expense. If you want to document improvement for utility rebate applications, qualify for an energy efficiency program, or get a precise pre- and post-repair comparison, a certified energy auditor's blower door test is necessary and worth the cost.
